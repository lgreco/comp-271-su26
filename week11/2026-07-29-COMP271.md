## Wrapping Up Hash Tables

On our last synchronous meeting of the term, we closed the loop on hash tables: an array of linked lists where a hash function locates the right slot in one step, and the list at that slot (capped at a handful of nodes) is walked in at most a few more, giving lookup in roughly constant time rather than scanning every element.

## Why Sums and Products Make Weak Hash Functions

We tested two hand-built hash functions against student last names — summing ASCII codes and multiplying them — and found both produce predictable periodicities (nearly all products came out even, since half the alphabet has an even ASCII value). Python's built-in string hash avoids this by using a polynomial with a prime base, which is why we call `abs()` on a hash code before using it: the underlying arithmetic can overflow and wrap around to a negative number, the same fixed-width overflow behavior that shows up whenever a computer's integer representation runs out of room (Python's own integers dodge this by falling back to string-based math once a number gets large enough).

## Insertion in Constant Time, and When to Resize

We fixed the earlier design flaw — inserting at the end of a list requires walking its full length — by always inserting new nodes at the head instead, making insertion $\mathcal O(1)$. This trades some unused array space for speed, and once occupancy passes a load factor threshold (commonly 75%), we resize and redistribute rather than trying to cap individual list lengths, since no algorithm guarantees a clean rebalance.

## Dictionaries as Hash Tables

We closed by naming Python's `dict` as exactly this structure under the hood — a key-value store nobody should reimplement by hand — and why hash-based lookup has underpinned database technology, largely unchanged, for over 50 years.

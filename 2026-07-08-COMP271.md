## Recursion and the factorial method

We revisited the factorial calculation, first writing it the "naive" way with an accumulator that starts at 1 and multiplies through a loop. Then we rewrote it recursively, using the definition $n! = n \times (n-1)!$, with $0! = 1$ as the base case. We pushed the recursive version to its limits, computing factorials of increasingly large numbers until Python raised a maximum recursion depth error — a natural bridge into why recursion depends on a stack.

## Recursion as a stack of pending requests

Using a role-play with several students passing a factorial request down a chain and passing answers back up, we modeled how each recursive call is a pending "order" placed on a stack, resolved last-in-first-out starting from the base case. We connected this to computer architecture: a dedicated stack register in the CPU tracks the next instruction to execute, which is why stacks are foundational to both recursion and iterative control flow. A second application — traversing a map of locations to see if a path exists — showed stacks used for backtracking through unexplored choices.

## Stack and queue operations

We defined the core operations: push/pop for stacks (last in, first out) and enqueue/dequeue for queues (first in, first out), plus peek (check the next item without removing it), size, and is-empty. With Kofi and Q. Dutch, we worked through how a queue backed by a fixed-size array requires shifting all remaining elements after a removal — an $\mathcal O(n)$ operation — versus the constant-time push/pop or enqueue/dequeue possible with a linked list, where we just move the head or tail pointer.
